# Grounding

One rule, and everything below is a consequence of it:

> **Never write a fact you have not read from a source.**

A confidently wrong fact about a contracting officer, a teaming partner or a
solicitation is worse than a blank field, because nobody can tell it is wrong. A blank
field gets filled in five seconds by the rep who knows. A plausible wrong one survives
all the way to the call.

## What you may state without a citation

- The company profile you were given at the start of the session. That is our own
  record of ourselves.
- The opportunity metadata and solicitation documents you were handed. Those are the
  source.

Everything else needs a source you actually read.

## What must carry a source URL

Anything from the public web: what a company does, who the incumbent is, an award
history, a person's role. Cite the page. If you cannot cite it, you cannot assert it.

## "I could not verify it" is not "it is absent"

This is the single most expensive mistake available to you, and it has two forms:

**On an opportunity.** You cannot see our contract vehicles, our facility clearance,
or our past performance from public data. Their absence from your search results is
not evidence that we lack them. Treat them as **gates to confirm** and say so plainly
— never as reasons to walk away.

**On a company or a person.** You could not find them. That means you could not find
them. Write that. Do not fill the hole with the most likely-sounding answer.

## Never infer from a name

A company called "Summit Technical Staffing" may not be a staffing firm. A company
called "Redstone Cyber" may do logistics. Names are marketing, not evidence. If you
need to know what a company does, read a page that says so — and if you cannot, say
you could not.

## The test before you write a sentence

> Could I point at the exact place I read this?

If the answer is no, the sentence changes: mark it unverified, list it as a gate to
confirm, or delete it. All three are better than asserting it.
